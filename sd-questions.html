39.4% at 2 hours, 40.7% at 4, 41.5% at 6. Each step up buys less than the last, because the gain from giving a CSP longer is partly cancelled by reroutes that no longer happen in time.

Why the response columns need no model

Every offer in the week is recorded with the exact time the CSP answered it. An offer answered after 3 hours 12 minutes sits in the data with that latency, under today's 6-hour window. Asking what the response rate would be at 4 hours is simply counting the offers whose recorded latency is under 4 hours. Nothing is projected. The same is true at 2 hours.

This only works downward from the live setting. It cannot tell us anything about 8 hours, because every offer is destroyed at 6.

The base is all 2,000 recorded offers. The replay uses a slightly narrower one — it stops walking a booking's chain once someone accepts, so offers that would never have been made are not counted. That is why the install columns are marked differently from the response columns even at 6 hours.

Why the install columns do need one

Shortening the window does not just cut responses off earlier — it changes when the next CSP gets the booking. That is what the replay models, and it is the only counterfactual in the exercise.

For each booking the actual chain of CSPs is walked in order. Each CSP's own response — accept, decline or silence — and his own latency are taken exactly as recorded. The single thing that changes is the moment a timeout fires: at 4 hours it fires two hours earlier than it did, so the next CSP receives the booking two hours earlier and is measured against the same deadline. A booking counts as served when some CSP accepts before noon on the day after the promised slot. The requeue lag is set to zero because that is what it measures — after a decline the next offer goes out in a median of 0.0 hours; after a timeout, 14.6 calendar hours, which is the timer itself and nothing else.

Served bookings are then converted to installs at the observed rates: 61.2% once served in time, 10.9% if never served. Those two rates are measured, not assumed.

What validates the replay

Run at 6 hours it must reproduce reality, and it does — to 0.1 points. 60.9% of bookings served against 60.8% actually served. That is the test that matters: the machinery is being asked to predict a world we can already see, and it lands on it.

Two further checks. The ranking of 2, 4 and 6 hours is unchanged under all three deadline definitions (end of the slot day, noon the day after, end of the day after). And the assumption that a CSP's speed is his own property rather than a reaction to the deadline holds: under the old 2-hour window and today's 6-hour window, the response curves inside the first two hours are near-parallel — 46.9% against 49.2% at the 2-hour mark, with no sign of a rush as the shorter deadline approaches.

The loss column is what separates system efficiency from one CSP's efficiency. Every booking in it was already being installed at the shorter setting — by a second CSP, after the first one timed out. Give the first CSP longer and that reroute starts later, and lands past the promised day. Checked individually: 5 of 5, 12 of 12 and 10 of 10 of the lost bookings had been rescued by a reroute at the shorter setting. Not one was lost for any other reason.

So the two forces are exactly the ones you described. Longer window, more first-pass acceptances. Longer window, fewer second chances. At 2→4 the trade is strongly favourable (+1.6 against −0.2). By 4→6 it has narrowed (+1.4 against −0.6) — the loss triples while the gain barely moves.

Correction — 8 hours cannot be evaluated on this data, and I previously said it could.

The gain column at 8 hours is structurally empty. P41 is 6 hours, so no CSP has ever been given the chance to answer in hour 7. The replay can see every booking that loses a reroute at 8 hours and none of the ones it would gain. The −0.5 is the loss half of a two-sided ledger.

Scaling the missing gain by the observed response decay — hours 4→6 added 3.4 points of response and rescued 29 bookings, hours 6→8 project 2.8 — suggests roughly 24 bookings gained, about +1.2, for a net near +0.7. On that basis 8 hours is probably still positive, not negative.

What the data establishes is the direction, not the turning point: gain per step flat to falling, loss per step rising. The curve flattens and will eventually turn over. Where it turns cannot be known without running a longer window and measuring it.

The limitation, and which way it cuts. The replay can only try CSPs who were actually offered the booking. Under a shorter window more reroutes would fit and we do not know who they would be — 296 bookings run out of chain at 2 hours against 198 at 6. Crediting one extra attempt at the observed 41.6% acceptance rate lifts every setting and moves the best to 3 hours. So these figures understate short settings, and the true optimum is at or below 6 hours rather than above it. Population is the 1,030 bookings carrying a confirmed slot date.

SD Q2. A CSP gets the same lead twice — what is the impact of that?

It almost never works, and it is expensive. In the same week 530 offers went back to a CSP who had already timed out on that booking or declined it. Twenty-two ended in an install — 4.2%.

Behaviour repeats itself. A CSP who ignored the booking ignores it again 79.2% of the time. One who declined it declines again 77.6% of the time, and that group produced a single install out of 170 attempts. Re-asking a CSP who has said no is as close to worthless as this data contains.

Why this matters for P41

Every one of those offers holds the booking for a full window before it can move on. Set against the runway measured in SD Q1 — a median of 15.5 working hours before the deadline — that is the scarcest thing in the system being spent on the least productive thing in it. Counting every repeat offer, including the 57 that went back to a CSP who had accepted and then failed downstream, the total is 587.

One booking in four is re-offered to a CSP who has already seen it. For those 400 bookings, 46% of every offer made is a repeat — and at six hours a window, that is roughly 3,500 working hours against the 6,200 those bookings collectively have. Well over half their runway goes to re-asking people who have already not acted.

Read the last table as a cost, not a cause. Bookings with repeats install at 5–8% against 53% for those without — but a booking gets re-offered because it is hard to place, so most of that gap is the difficulty, not the repeating. The causal claim is not available here.

What is defensible is the arithmetic: 587 windows were consumed and 25 installs came back, 530 of those windows going to a CSP who had already timed out or declined. That holds whatever the underlying difficulty, because the windows were spent either way.

The implication is not a change to P41's length. It is that the same runway would be worth more if a CSP who has already timed out or declined were not asked again while an unasked CSP is available. That is a routing rule rather than a timer value — and it would make any P41 setting go further, because it stops the window being spent on the 79% who will not answer twice.

SD Q3. Why is P95 not the right cut-off, given the lead can go to someone else and/or come back again?

Because P95 is not a measurement of CSP behaviour — it is a measurement of the current setting. Using it to choose the setting is circular.

The response window has been at 2 hours and at 6. Here is what the response-time distribution looked like under each:

Window in forceResponsesP50P90 P95P95 as a share of the window
2 hours (12–19 Jul)2,689 1 min1h 07m1h 34m79%
6 hours (26 Jul – 1 Aug)2,274 2 min3h 27m5h 09m86%

P95 tripled when the window tripled. It sits at roughly four-fifths of whatever it is given, in both regimes. Meanwhile P50 barely moved — one minute to two. The median is a real property of how CSPs behave; the tail is a property of how long we let them take.

So "set P41 to cover P95" has no fixed point. Set it to 6 and P95 becomes 5. Set it to 8 and P95 becomes about 7. The rule would justify any number it was pointed at.

And the lead is not lost when the window closes

The second half of the question matters just as much. P95 answers "how long until nearly every CSP who will ever answer has answered" — but a CSP who does not answer is not the end of the booking. It goes to someone else, and it can come back. A booking whose first CSP never answers still installs 18.8% of the time (SD Q1).

That is why covering the tail is worth less than it looks: the last few percent of responses are being bought at the cost of delaying the reroute for everyone who was never going to answer at all. SD Q1 measures that trade directly — at 4→6 hours it is +1.4 points gained against −0.6 lost.

What should set the cut-off instead

The customer's own deadline. Cancellation is near-zero until the promised slot day arrives and then steps up sharply, peaking the afternoon after it. Working back from that point, the median booking has 15.5 working hours and passes through 1.9 CSPs — which is where the ~8 working hours recommendation in Part 3 comes from.

That is a guardrail derived from the customer, not from the parameter's own past values. It cannot be gamed by changing the setting, which is exactly what P95 can.

One qualification the simulation adds. Runway ÷ chain prices the window but not the reroute the window delays. SD Q1 prices both, and finds the return on each extra pair of hours falling — +1.3 points from 2 to 4, +0.8 from 4 to 6 — while the loss from delayed reroutes triples across the same span.

It does not show 8 hours to be worse; that cannot be measured, because no CSP has ever been given the chance to answer in hour 7. But it does mean 8 should be read as the top of a flattening curve rather than a target with room above it.
