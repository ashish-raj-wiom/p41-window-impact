Questions raised on the P41 analysis, and what the data says to each.

SD Q1 supersedes the recommendation in the main analysis. It simulates total system efficiency and finds 6 working hours is the optimum and 8 is worse. Part 3 of the analysis still argues for 8, on a runway ÷ chain calculation that does not price the window inflating the cost of every failed attempt. Part 3 has deliberately been left unchanged.

SD Q1. What is total system efficiency of install for P41 at 2 hour, 4 hour and 6 hour? Total efficiency is different from one CSP efficiency.

39.4%, 40.7% and 41.5%. And at 8 hours it falls back to 41.0%.

Total system efficiency is not one CSP's efficiency, and the difference is the whole answer. Getting it needs a simulation, because the reroute is not a fixed fallback: a shorter window sends the booking to the next CSP earlier, with more runway left, and the rescue rate depends on exactly that.

Read the 8-hour row across. Response rate and positive response rate are the best in the table — the individual CSP keeps improving with more time. But bookings served before the deadline fall, 60.9% to 59.9%, and overall install falls with them. At 8 hours every failed attempt burns 8 working hours of runway instead of 6, so bookings needing a second CSP run past the promised day: past-deadline failures rise from 205 to 266. More answers per offer, fewer bookings served.

How it is built. Each booking's actual CSP chain is replayed under a candidate P41. Every CSP's own response — accept, decline or silence — and his own latency are taken as observed; the only counterfactual is where the window truncates. Requeue lag is set to zero because that is what it measures: after a decline the next offer goes out in a median of 0.0 hours, after a timeout in 14.6 calendar hours, which is the timer itself. Population is the 1,030 bookings carrying a confirmed slot date.

Three checks it passes. At 6 hours it reproduces observed reality to 0.1 points — 60.9% served against 60.8% actual. The optimum stays at 6 hours under all three deadline definitions (end of the slot day, noon the day after, end of the day after). And the truncation assumption holds: response curves inside the first two hours are near-parallel between the 2-hour and 6-hour regimes, so CSPs are not pacing themselves to the deadline.

The limitation, and which way it cuts. The simulation can only try CSPs who were actually offered the booking. Under a shorter window more reroutes would fit and we do not know who they would be — 296 bookings run out of chain at 2 hours against 198 at 6. Crediting one extra attempt at the observed 41.6% acceptance rate moves the optimum to 3 hours (45.6%). So the optimum is 6 hours if the reroute pool is shallow and 3 hours if it is deep — but 8 hours is worse than both in every specification run.

SD Q2. Is all the data from the same time window?

Now yes — it was not. Two problems, both fixed.

The cancellation group was labelled 25 Jun – 30 Jul. Its real range is 22–30 Jul: customer-proposed slots barely existed before the flow change, so the lower bound never bound. The guardrail was already measured entirely on the new regime — the label understated how clean it was.

The age-of-booking curve genuinely was from the old regime — bookings confirmed 1–20 July, before both the flow change and the P41 change, and across all slot sources. Re-cut on customer-proposed bookings offered 22–28 Jul with a full 7 days each. Same conclusion: 2.8% cancel inside the first six hours, then +0.5 points to hour 12.

SD Q3. Should cancellations exclude bookings where a technician was already assigned?

Conceptually yes, but the filter cannot be read cleanly, and the decision does not change.

Only 224 of 981 bookings (23%) never got a technician. Restricting to them cuts the evidence from 139 cancellations in the plotted window to 56, and the largest bucket moves to +3 afternoon — on 10 cancellations, against 7, 7 and 7 in the next three buckets. That is not a peak.

It also emerges that the sharp +1 peak is driven mainly by bookings that did get a technician — 17 of those 24. Which is a fair hit on the framing.

But the filter conditions on an outcome downstream of the timing being measured: a booking cancelled on day 0 can never be assigned afterwards, so it keeps early cancellers and drops late ones. The rates show it — never-assigned bookings cancel at 25.0% against 15.3% for assigned. Either way the deadline moves later under the restriction, which affords more runway, not less.

SD Q5. "First response arrived" — is that a positive response?

No — it was any response, accept or decline, and that was hiding the finding. The latency used was the earlier of technician-assigned and declined, so a fast decline sat in the same band as a fast acceptance. Split apart:

The booking-level view had appeared to slope downward; that was the rising share of declines dragging the blend down, not late acceptances converting worse.

SD Q6. Pre/post showed a much better response rate. Why not lean on it?

Because +12.2 points cannot be attributed to P41. Response went 46.9% → 59.1% and jobs taken 33.2% → 39.0% between 12–19 Jul and 26 Jul – 1 Aug. Three reasons it cannot carry the case.

Several things went live at once, the customer-scheduling flow among them, and a before/after gap cannot be attributed to any one of them. Held to a common 2-hour cut-off the two weeks are 46.9% against 49.2% — 2.3 points apart, so most of the headline is simply the window being longer. And the two weeks measure different actions: proposing a slot then, assigning a technician now.
