P41 — calibrating the CSP acceptance window

What the parameter should be set to, and why.

Currently 6 working hours Response data 26 Jul – 1 Aug 2026 Cancellation data 25 Jun – 30 Jul 2026 All times IST

What P41 is for

When a booking is allocated to a CSP, P41 is the window he has to respond — either take the job or decline it. If he does neither, P41 fires, the booking is released, and the system goes looking for another CSP who might do the work.

So the parameter sits on a single trade-off, and calibrating it means answering two questions:

  • How much time is enough for the CSP to act? Too short and we pull away bookings the CSP was about to take, and reroute work that never needed rerouting.
  • How long can the customer be kept waiting? Too long and the booking sits with someone who was never going to act, while the customer waits and the search for a replacement is delayed.

Recommendation

Keep P41 at 6 working hours as the default.

The binding constraint is not customer impatience by the clock — it is the promised slot day. 90% of customer cancellations happen on or after that day, peaking the day after. So the allocation chain has to finish before the slot day ends.

The median booking has 12.8 working hours of runway to that deadline and passes through 2 CSPs. That implies 12.8 ÷ 2 ≈ 6.4 working hours per CSP — the median booking can be offered, time out, be re-offered, and still be installed on the day it was promised. Six hours sits just inside that.

It also comfortably covers CSP behaviour: ranked into quartiles by their own response rate, every quartile of CSPs has a P95 response inside 5 hours — 3h 24m for the best, 4h 52m for the worst. Six hours captures essentially everyone who was ever going to respond.

But cut it to ~5 hours for same-day slots.

The runway is not the same for every booking. For the 65% promised a next-day slot it is 16.5 working hours and 6h is comfortable. For the 24% promised a same-day slot it is only 9.9 working hours — which fits just 1.7 P41 cycles. One timeout consumes the runway and any reroute lands after the day the customer was promised.

The strongest version of this: make P41 a function of the runway the booking actually has — slot date minus now, divided by the expected chain length — rather than a constant. A single global number is currently doing two different jobs, and doing one of them badly.

And shorten it for CSPs who never respond.

The bottom quartile responds to just 5.1% of bookings even given the full six hours — and 3.3% of that arrives within two. Cutting their window to 2h would cost about 10 tasks a week while releasing roughly 526 bookings four working hours sooner, buying back runway for the reroute.

Why

A note on method: this looks only at the current regime

All figures below are drawn from the period after P41 moved to 6 hours. This is deliberately not a before/after comparison, for two reasons:

1. Several changes shipped at once. The customer-scheduling flow went live ~21 July, one day before the P41 change. Any pre/post difference mixes the two, and neither can be isolated cleanly.

2. Changing the timer changes the behaviour being measured. Under a 2-hour window you cannot observe a five-hour response — the task was already dead. The old regime's numbers are truncated by its own deadline, so comparing them to the new regime's is not like-for-like. Calibration needs the distribution of what CSPs actually do when given room, which only the current regime shows.

Every extra hour buys more response, and it does so at a near-constant rate. The first hour is the steep one; from hour 2 onward each additional hour adds roughly the same amount again — 2.1pp/h overall, and the bottom row shows the same steady climb inside every quartile. There is no point in the range where the curve flattens out and the next hour stops paying.

The shape is the same in every quartile; the size is not. Q1, Q2 and Q3 all gain 2–3.5pp per hour. Q4 gains 0.5pp per hour — a seventh of Q2's rate. Waiting buys real response from CSPs who engage at all, and almost nothing from those who do not. That asymmetry is the whole basis for the third recommendation.

What the move from 2h to 6h was worth. Response rose 54.1% → 62.6% (+8.5pp) and jobs taken 37.2% → 41.8% (+4.6pp). Converting the task-level gain to bookings:

+4.6pp across 3,165 tasks ≈ 146 additional first-pass assignments. But a task that times out is not a booking lost — 44.2% of connections whose CSP never responded still get a technician from a later CSP. So only 55.8% of those 146 are genuinely incremental: ≈81 connections against the cohort's 1,654, or +4.9pp of booking-level supply efficiency. That is what a return to 2 hours would give back.

5. Establishing the second half: more time also costs cancellations — but only past one pivot

If waiting only ever bought response, the answer would be to set P41 very long. It does not: the customer is running a clock of his own. The question is what that clock is measuring, because it is not hours since booking.

Cancellations in the first few hours are not impatience — they are bookings the customer never intended to keep. 3.3% cancel inside the first six hours, then the curve almost stops: only +0.5pp more across the whole of hours 6 to 12. Those early ones would have cancelled whatever P41 was set to, so they should not be priced as a cost of waiting.

5a. When cancellation actually happens, relative to the promised slot

Re-cutting the same cancellations against the customer's promised slot date rather than the booking time shows what he is really waiting for. All 981 customer-proposed connections whose slot is now at least five days past, pooled — no split yet — in 12-hour buckets:

So the customer is not counting hours since booking. He is waiting for the day he was promised, and gives up in the evening once it has gone by. The pivot is the evening of the day after the slot — the largest single bucket anywhere in the window — and that is the point the allocation has to finish before.

Stated precisely, because the 12-hour view makes it visible: summed to whole days, the slot day and the day after are level pooled (3.06% each). What separates them is concentration — the day after puts 80% of its mass in one evening bucket, while the slot day spreads its evenly. And for next-day slots, which are the majority, the day after is decisively larger (4.17% against 2.46%).

That matters for calibration as much as the cancellation shape does: the runway P41 has to fit inside is short for essentially the entire base, not just a tail. It also caps how much this section can say about long lead times — 2- and 3-day slots are 44 connections and 5 cancellations between them, which is a count, not a rate.

Customer-proposed slots only, one row per connection: its first candidate and the slot promised at that point, so re-slotting and rerouting do not double-count. Only slots at least five days past as of 4 Aug are included. The behaviour being measured — customers give up when the promised day passes — is not specific to the P41 window, which is why the wider window is acceptable here.

6. Putting the two halves together

Section 4 established: every extra hour of P41 buys more response, at a near-constant 2.1pp/h, in every quartile of CSPs. Nothing in the range 1h–6h flattens out. On its own, that argues for a longer window.

Section 5 established: the offsetting cost is not paid by the hour — it is paid at a pivot, the day after the promised slot. 90% of customer cancellations land on or after the promised day, and next-day slots peak the day after it.

So the two do not trade off smoothly. Extra hours are close to free right up until the allocation stops fitting inside the slot day, and expensive immediately after. The parameter should therefore be set to the largest value that still lets the median allocation conclude before that pivot — not to the point where marginal response gain equals marginal cancellation cost, because that point does not exist.

That turns the setting into arithmetic on three measurements:

InputValueHow it is measured
Runway — time from the booking first reaching a CSP to the end of the promised slot day 12.8 working hmedian; 24.8 calendar hours
Chain — distinct CSPs a connection passes through 2median; mean 1.91
Implied P41 = runway ÷ chain 6.4 working hso the median booking survives one reroute and still makes its slot

The current setting of six working hours sits just inside that. Two CSPs at 6h consume 12 working hours against a 12.8-hour median runway — the median booking can be offered, time out, be re-offered, and still be installed on the day it was promised, ahead of the pivot. Six hours is therefore close to the largest value that satisfies the constraint; it is not a compromise between two smoothly opposing costs, because section 5 showed there is no such smooth trade-off to split.

7. How many CSP attempts actually fit before that deadline

Each CSP given the booking consumes one full P41 window before the system can try the next. So the question is simply: how many 6-hour windows fit in the runway before the promised slot day runs out?

"CSP attempts that fit" is just runway ÷ 6 — how many complete 6-hour windows the booking has room for before the slot day ends. 1.7 means one CSP gets a full window and the next only gets 70% of one before the day is gone.

So a single global number is doing two different jobs. The strongest version of the recommendation: make P41 a function of the runway the booking actually has — slot date minus now, divided by the chain length you want to be able to absorb — rather than a constant.

8. Time of day makes no difference

Because P41 runs on a working-hours clock (9 AM – 9 PM, pausing overnight), a booking arriving at 8 PM gets the same effective budget as one arriving at 10 AM. The data confirms the clock is doing its job:

Definitions
  • Working hours — 9 AM – 9 PM IST, counted every day of the week. This is P41's native unit: measured on that clock the window is exactly 6.00 hours, with minimum, median and maximum identical.
  • Response — the first of either a positive action or a decline by the CSP. The two are mutually exclusive and sum to the response rate.
  • Positive action — technician assignment. In the customer-proposed-slot flow the slot is already confirmed when the booking reaches the CSP, so assigning a technician is his first required step.
  • Population — install candidates on customer-proposed-slot bookings created 26 Jul – 1 Aug 2026 (n=3,165 across 1,654 connections). Quartile analysis restricted to CSPs with ≥5 bookings in the week (237 CSPs, 2,142 bookings).
  • Cancellation — distinct mobiles with a customer-initiated cancelled event, measured from booking confirmation. Bookings confirmed 21 Jul – 2 Aug so every one has ≥24h of observation.
