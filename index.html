P41 — calibrating the CSP acceptance window

What the parameter should be set to, and why.

Currently 6 working hours Data 26 Jul – 3 Aug 2026 All times IST

What P41 is for

When a booking is allocated to a CSP, P41 is the window he has to respond — either take the job or decline it. If he does neither, P41 fires, the booking is released, and the system goes looking for another CSP who might do the work.

So the parameter sits on a single trade-off, and calibrating it means answering two questions:

  • How much time is enough for the CSP to act? Too short and we pull away bookings the CSP was about to take, and reroute work that never needed rerouting.
  • How long can the customer be kept waiting? Too long and the booking sits with someone who was never going to act, while the customer waits and the search for a replacement is delayed.

Recommendation

Keep P41 at 6 working hours as the default.

The binding constraint is not customer impatience by the clock — it is the promised slot day. 69% of customer cancellations happen only after that day has passed, peaking the day after. So the allocation chain has to finish before the slot day ends.

The median booking has 12.8 working hours of runway to that deadline and passes through 2 CSPs. That implies 12.8 ÷ 2 ≈ 6.4 working hours per CSP — the median booking can be offered, time out, be re-offered, and still be installed on the day it was promised. Six hours sits just inside that.

It also comfortably covers CSP behaviour: P95 response is 4h 52m in the worst quartile and 3h 24m in the best, so 6 hours captures essentially everyone who was ever going to respond.

But cut it to ~5 hours for same-day slots.

The runway is not the same for every booking. For the 65% promised a next-day slot it is 16.5 working hours and 6h is comfortable. For the 24% promised a same-day slot it is only 9.9 working hours — which fits just 1.7 P41 cycles. One timeout consumes the runway and any reroute lands after the day the customer was promised.

The strongest version of this: make P41 a function of the runway the booking actually has — slot date minus now, divided by the expected chain length — rather than a constant. A single global number is currently doing two different jobs, and doing one of them badly.

And shorten it for CSPs who never respond.

The bottom quartile responds to just 5.2% of bookings even given the full six hours — and 3.1% of that arrives within two. Cutting their window to 2h would cost about 11 tasks a week while releasing roughly 520 bookings four working hours sooner, buying back runway for the reroute.

Why

A note on method: this looks only at the current regime

All figures below are drawn from the period after P41 moved to 6 hours. This is deliberately not a before/after comparison, for two reasons:

1. Several changes shipped at once. The customer-scheduling flow went live ~21 July, one day before the P41 change. Any pre/post difference mixes the two, and neither can be isolated cleanly.

2. Changing the timer changes the behaviour being measured. Under a 2-hour window you cannot observe a five-hour response — the task was already dead. The old regime's numbers are truncated by its own deadline, so comparing them to the new regime's is not like-for-like. Calibration needs the distribution of what CSPs actually do when given room, which only the current regime shows.

4. The real deadline is the promised slot, not the clock

Cancellations in the first few hours of a booking are not impatience — they are bookings the customer never intended to keep. The hazard is 0.55%/hour in the first six hours, then collapses to 0.08%/hour between hours 6 and 12. Those early ones would have cancelled whatever P41 was set to, so they should not be priced as a cost of waiting.

Re-cutting the same cancellations against the customer's promised slot date rather than the booking time shows what they are actually waiting for:

The customer is not counting hours since booking — he is waiting for the day he was promised, and giving up once it goes by. That is the guardrail: the allocation must finish before the end of the slot day.

5. Calibrating against that guardrail

If the slot day is the deadline, the parameter follows from three measurements:

InputValueHow it is measured
Runway — time from the booking first reaching a CSP to the end of the promised slot day 12.8 working hmedian; 24.8 calendar hours
Chain — distinct CSPs a connection passes through 2median; mean 1.91
Implied P41 = runway ÷ chain 6.4 working hso the median booking survives one reroute and still makes its slot

The current setting of six working hours sits just inside that. Two CSPs at 6h consume 12 working hours against a 12.8-hour median runway — the median booking can be offered, time out, be re-offered, and still be installed on the day it was promised. That is the case for 6 hours, and it is a tighter one than a marginal cost-benefit ratio.

6. Where 6 hours is too generous

The runway is not the same for everyone. It depends entirely on how far ahead the promised slot is:

For the 65% of bookings promised a next-day slot, six hours is comfortable — the runway absorbs almost three full P41 cycles. For the 24% promised a same-day slot, six hours is too long: the runway only stretches to 1.7 cycles, so a single timeout consumes most of it and any reroute lands after the promised day. Those bookings need roughly 5 working hours to preserve a two-CSP chain, and arguably less.

This is the same conclusion the CSP quartiles pointed at, reached from the customer's side: a single global number is doing two jobs badly. The strongest version of this recommendation is to make P41 a function of the runway the booking actually has — slot date minus now — rather than a constant.

7. Time of day makes no difference

Because P41 runs on a working-hours clock (9 AM – 9 PM, pausing overnight), a booking arriving at 8 PM gets the same effective budget as one arriving at 10 AM. The data confirms the clock is doing its job:

Definitions
  • Working hours — 9 AM – 9 PM IST, counted every day of the week. This is P41's native unit: measured on that clock the window is exactly 6.00 hours, with minimum, median and maximum identical.
  • Response — the first of either a positive action or a decline by the CSP. The two are mutually exclusive and sum to the response rate.
  • Positive action — technician assignment. In the customer-proposed-slot flow the slot is already confirmed when the booking reaches the CSP, so assigning a technician is his first required step.
  • Population — install candidates on customer-proposed-slot bookings created 26 Jul – 1 Aug 2026 (n=3,165 across 1,654 connections). Quartile analysis restricted to CSPs with ≥5 bookings in the week (237 CSPs, 2,142 bookings).
  • Cancellation — distinct mobiles with a customer-initiated cancelled event, measured from booking confirmation. Bookings confirmed 21 Jul – 2 Aug so every one has ≥24h of observation.
