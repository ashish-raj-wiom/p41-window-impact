P41 — calibrating the CSP acceptance window

What the parameter should be set to, and why.

Currently 6 working hours Response data 26 Jul – 1 Aug 2026 Cancellation data 25 Jun – 30 Jul 2026 Deadline: evening after the promised day All times IST

What P41 is for

When a booking is allocated to a CSP, P41 is the window he has to respond — either take the job or decline it. If he does neither, P41 fires, the booking is released, and the system goes looking for another CSP who might do the work.

So the parameter sits on a single trade-off, and calibrating it means answering two questions:

  • How much time is enough for the CSP to act? Too short and we pull away bookings the CSP was about to take, and reroute work that never needed rerouting.
  • How long can the customer be kept waiting? Too long and the booking sits with someone who was never going to act, while the customer waits and the search for a replacement is delayed.

Recommendation

Raise P41 from 6 to 8 working hours.

The deadline that matters is the evening after the promised day — that is when customers give up and cancel. Working back from it, the average booking has 15.5 working hours before it — about a day and a half of real time — and passes through 1.9 CSPs. That leaves 8 working hours per CSP.

Six hours leaves two of those hours unused. CSPs were still accepting jobs at a steady rate when the current window cut them off, so the extra time should convert: we expect roughly +3 points of response and +1.5 points of jobs accepted, worth about +1.6 points on installs per booking.

Those three figures are a projection, not a measurement — see the caveat in section 6. The 8-hour figure itself is measured.

Why

Three steps. Part 1 asks whether a longer window actually buys more response. Part 2 asks what limits how long it can be. Part 3 puts the two together and gets the number. Each part opens with its conclusion, then the evidence behind it.

Part 1 of 3

Does a longer window buy more response?

Yes — and it was still paying when the current 6-hour window cut it off.

Response climbs steadily: roughly 2 points for every extra hour from hour 2 onward, and the same pattern holds inside every group of CSPs. Nothing between 1 and 6 hours flattens out — there is no point where the next hour stops earning.

What differs between CSPs is not how fast they answer but whether they answer at all: from 100% in the best quarter to 5% in the worst. Waiting longer does not close that gap.

On its own, this argues for a longer window. Points 1 to 4 below.

A CSP who is going to take the job does it almost immediately; the later hours of the window are mostly spent waiting on people who will eventually say no, or nothing at all.

Every extra hour buys more response, and it does so at a near-constant rate. The first hour is the steep one; from hour 2 onward each additional hour adds roughly the same amount again — 2.1 points/h overall, and the bottom row shows the same steady climb inside every quarter. The shape is the same in every quarter; the size is not. The best three groups gain 2.2–3.5 points per hour. The weakest gains 0.4 points per hour — a ninth of the second group's rate. Waiting buys real response from CSPs who engage at all, and almost nothing from those who do not.

What the move from 2h to 6h was worth. Response rose 54.1% → 62.6% (+8.5 points) and jobs taken 37.2% → 41.8% (+4.6 points). Converting the task-level gain to bookings:

+4.6 points across 3,165 tasks ≈ 146 additional first-pass assignments. But a task that times out is not a booking lost — 44.2% of bookings whose CSP never responded still get a technician from a later CSP. So only 55.8% of those 146 are genuinely incremental: ≈81 bookings against the group's 1,654, or +4.9 points of installs per booking. That is what a return to 2 hours would give back.

Part 2 of 3

So what stops us making it longer?

A deadline — not a cost that grows by the hour.

Customers do not get impatient hour by hour. They wait for the day they were promised, and give up on the evening after it. Cancellation is near zero right up to that point and jumps the moment it arrives.

That fixes the point the allocation has to finish before. And it is the same point whichever day the customer chose — same-day and next-day bookings behave identically once each is lined up against its own promised date.

Extra hours are close to free until that deadline, and expensive after it. Point 5 below.

5. When customers give up

If waiting only ever bought response, the answer would be to set P41 very long. It does not: the customer is running a clock of his own. The question is what that clock is measuring, because it is not hours since booking.

Cancellations in the first few hours are not impatience — they are bookings the customer never intended to keep. 3.3% cancel inside the first six hours, then the curve almost stops: only +0.5 points more across the whole of hours 6 to 12. Those early ones would have cancelled whatever P41 was set to, so they should not be priced as a cost of waiting.

Re-cutting the same cancellations against the customer's promised slot date rather than the booking time shows what he is really waiting for. All 981 customer-proposed bookings whose slot is now at least five days past, pooled — no split yet — in 12-hour buckets:

So the customer is not counting hours since booking. He is waiting for the day he was promised, and gives up in the evening once it has gone by. The deadline is the evening of the day after the slot — the largest single bucket anywhere in the window — and that is the point the allocation has to finish before.

Stated precisely, because the 12-hour view makes it visible: summed to whole days, the slot day and the day after are level pooled (3.06% each). What separates them is concentration — the day after puts 80% of its mass in one evening bucket, while the slot day spreads its evenly. And for the next-day-or-later group plotted below, the day after is decisively larger (3.85% against 2.45%).

5b. It does not matter which day they chose

Splitting the same 981 bookings by the slot they proposed, with the two combined shown alongside — everything else identical:

That is the whole purpose of this split. It rules out the objection that the deadline is an artefact of slot mix — that the peak sits where it does only because most customers happen to choose the next day. It does not: each group pivots on its own promised slot. The same-day and next-day groups behave identically once you line them up on the day they were promised.

With the deadline established and shown to be independent of the slot chosen, the split has done its job and the calibration does not need it. Section 6 works on the whole group and a single median.

The 44 bookings promised a slot two or more days out are folded into "next day or later" — on their own they carry 5 cancellations between them, a count rather than a rate.

Two groups — read them separately. This section runs on the cancellation group (981 bookings, 25 Jun – 30 Jul, matured), because cancellation needs the slot to be several days past before it can be counted. Section 6 runs on the response group (1,654 bookings, 26 Jul – 1 Aug), the same population as sections 1–4.

What carries across between them is the behaviour, not the numbers: cancellation turns on the promised slot whatever slot was chosen, and that holds in both. Section 6 takes only the deadline from here, and measures its own time-available on its own population.

Population for this section. Unlike the response analysis above (26 Jul – 1 Aug), the cancellation group runs 25 Jun – 30 Jul 2026 — cancellation is a slow signal and needs the slot to be several days in the past before it can be counted, so the POST-only window is too recent to supply it. Customer-proposed slots only, one row per booking: its first candidate and the slot promised at that point, so re-slotting and rerouting do not double-count. Only slots at least five days past as of 4 Aug are included. The behaviour being measured — customers give up when the promised day passes — is not specific to the window, which is why the wider window is acceptable here.

Part 3 of 3

So what should P41 be?

8 working hours. We are at 6.

Part 1 says take as much time as the deadline allows. Part 2 says where the deadline is. The setting falls out of the two: 15.5 working hours available ÷ 1.9 CSPs tried = 8 working hours each.

Point 6 below sets out the measurement, what the extra two hours should be worth, and why that last figure is an estimate rather than something we can read off the data.

6. The calculation

Section 4 established: every extra hour of P41 buys more response, at a near-constant 2.1 points/h, in every quarter of CSPs. Nothing in the range 1h–6h flattens out. On its own, that argues for a longer window.

Section 5 established: the offsetting cost is not paid by the hour — it lands in one bucket, the afternoon and evening of the day after the promised slot. That is the largest single bucket anywhere in the window, and it is where the customer stops waiting. The deadline is the afternoon and evening of the day after the promised slot.

So the two do not trade off smoothly. Extra hours are close to free right up until the allocation stops fitting ahead of that pivot, and expensive after. The parameter should be set to the largest value that still lets the median allocation conclude before it — not to the point where marginal response gain equals marginal cancellation cost, because that point does not exist.

Working back from that deadline — the evening after the promised day — across all 1,654 bookings in the week:

What we measureValueHow
Time available — from the booking reaching its first CSP to the deadline 15.5 working hours
39.5 calendar hours		 the middle booking — about a day and a half of real time, which is 15.5 hours once the overnight gap is taken out
CSPs tried — how many get the booking before it is done 1.9 average per booking; each one holds it for a full window
Time per CSP = available ÷ tried 8 working hours the setting at which the typical booking still finishes ahead of the deadline

So the answer is 8 working hours, and we are currently at 6. Two hours are being left on the table.

What the extra two hours should buy — and why this part is an estimate.

We cannot measure it directly. The timer currently kills a booking at 6 hours, so no CSP has ever had the chance to answer in hour 7. The response curve looks flat after 6 hours only because that is where it is cut off, not because CSPs stop responding.

What we can see is the rate at which acceptances were still arriving when the window closed: +2.1, +1.8 and +1.6 points in the last three hours before the cut-off — slowing, but nowhere near stopped. Carrying that same slowdown two hours further gives +2.8 points of response and +1.5 points of jobs accepted, worth about +1.6 points on installs per booking.

Treat that as the central case. If the slowdown is steeper than assumed the gain is smaller; if response held at its recent pace it would be closer to +4 points. The direction is not in doubt — only the size.

One caveat on coverage. 226 of the 1,654 bookings (one in seven) reach a CSP too late in the day for even a single window to close before the deadline. No timer value reaches them, at 2 hours or at 12. Their problem is when they enter the queue relative to the day they were promised, not the length of the window.

Definitions
  • Working hours — 9am–9pm IST, counted every day of the week. This is P41's native unit: measured on that clock the window is exactly 6.00 hours, with minimum, median and maximum identical.
  • Response — the first of either a accepting the job or a decline by the CSP. The two are mutually exclusive and sum to the response rate.
  • Accepting the job — technician assignment. In the customer-proposed-slot flow the slot is already confirmed when the booking reaches the CSP, so assigning a technician is his first required step.
  • Population — bookings offered to a CSP on customer-proposed-slot bookings created 26 Jul – 1 Aug 2026 (n=3,165 across 1,654 bookings). Quartile analysis restricted to CSPs with ≥5 bookings in the week (237 CSPs, 2,142 bookings).
  • Cancellation — a customer-initiated cancelled event. Two cuts are used, on different groups.
